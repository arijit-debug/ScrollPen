How plants make food: photosynthesis

8 min read · Updated for this term

Plants do not eat. Instead, they make their own food out of light, water and air. This process is called photosynthesis, and it happens mostly in the leaves, inside tiny green parts of the cell called chloroplasts.

Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, the pigment that makes leaves green. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light very well, and reflects green light, which is why we see plants as green.

6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂

In words: carbon dioxide and water, using the energy of light, become glucose (a sugar) and oxygen. The plant uses the glucose for energy and to build its body. The oxygen is released into the air, which is good news for us.

The two stages

Photosynthesis happens in two stages. In the light-dependent reactions, energy from light is captured and water molecules are split, releasing oxygen. In the Calvin cycle, that captured energy is used to turn carbon dioxide into glucose.

The first stage needs light. The second stage does not need light directly, but it depends on the energy made in the first stage, so in practice both stages run during the day.

What affects the speed?

Three things limit how fast photosynthesis can go: the amount of light, the amount of carbon dioxide, and the temperature. Whichever one is in shortest supply sets the limit; this is called the limiting factor.

Too hot, and the enzymes that drive the Calvin cycle stop working properly. Too cold, and every reaction slows down.

Why it matters

Almost every food chain on Earth starts with photosynthesis. The oxygen in the air we breathe was made by it, and so was the energy stored in coal, oil and gas, millions of years ago.
